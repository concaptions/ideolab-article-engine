1. Executive ICP Summary
The established owner who needs a content engine
The core Content Velocity customer is an established business owner, founder, expert, practitioner, local operator, consultant, professional service provider, or product-brand owner whose real-world credibility is stronger than their online visibility. They are not inventing a business from scratch. They already have something valuable: expertise, customers, a product, a reputation, a referral network, or years of practical experience.
Their problem is translation. What they know and what their business does well are not consistently showing up in articles, social posts, videos, search results, or other digital touchpoints. They often know content matters, but content production loses to the daily demands of running the business.
The simplest public definition
Credible but under-visible. Experienced but overwhelmed. Interested in AI but protective of authenticity. Ready for content leverage, but not interested in becoming a full-time content technician.
Business stage
Operating business with a real offer, real customers, and existing market credibility.
Core problem
Inconsistent visibility and content execution; offline authority is not fully visible online.
Core desire
A repeatable system that turns expertise into useful, trustworthy, brand-aligned content.
Role they want
Expert and approver — not the person doing every step of content production.
Emotional need
Confidence that the content will sound accurate, feel authentic, and represent the business well.
Marketing lens
Lead with credibility, visibility, consistency, authenticity, and implementation — not AI novelty.

2. Master Ideal Customer Profile
A real business with real authority that needs to become more visible
The master ICP is intentionally broader than a single industry. Content Velocity is most relevant where expertise, trust, education, reputation, or product story influence the buying decision. The unifying trait is not what the company sells; it is that the company becomes easier to find, trust, and choose when its knowledge and credibility are turned into consistent content.
Common characteristics
An existing business, practice, consultancy, agency, local service, professional firm, product company, or expert-led brand.
A clear product, service, offer, or specialized area of expertise.
Some existing reputation, customers, referrals, case experience, or market credibility.
A need to become easier to find, understand, and trust online.
Enough business maturity to understand that marketing consistency matters.
A preference for simplification and implementation over another disconnected marketing task.
A willingness to provide expertise, context, feedback, and approval while delegating much of the production work.
The customer is usually not asking content to rescue a weak business. They are asking content to reveal, explain, and amplify a business that already has substance.
Public-facing one-sentence ICP
Content Velocity is best suited to established owners and expert-led brands that have real value to offer, know they need a stronger digital presence, and want a practical content system that turns what they already know into consistent, authentic visibility without forcing them to do everything manually.

What the business typically looks like before Content Velocity enters the picture
Business maturity

Decision-maker

Experience

Best-fit business categories
Professional services and high-trust experts

Local and regional service businesses
Real estate-related businesses

Family-owned product or service businesses
Mission-driven brands and cause-led founders
High-ticket B2B professionals and established entrepreneurs
The public marketing partner should avoid assuming that “older” means unsophisticated. The important distinction is experience plus limited time, not age alone. Many buyers understand exactly why content matters; they simply lack the bandwidth, systems, or desire to execute it themselves.

4. Core Psychographics
How the customer thinks about visibility, content, AI, and control
The Content Velocity buyer is practical, reputation-conscious, and outcome-oriented. They are rarely chasing technology for its own sake. AI may attract their attention, but ease, trust, authenticity, control, and consistent execution determine whether they actually want help.
Common internal beliefs
“I know I need to be more visible.”
“I want people to understand and trust me before they talk to me.”
“I want content, but I do not want to become a content creator.”
“If this does not sound like me, I will not use it.”
“I do not want another agency or tool that creates more work for me.”
“I am willing to review and approve, but I do not want to build the entire machine.”
“My business should look as credible online as it is in real life.”
“I want leverage, not more disconnected tasks.”
Emotional pattern
The emotional state is usually a blend of urgency, frustration, skepticism, and hope. They know the market has changed. Search, social, video, AI-assisted discovery, and digital trust increasingly shape how buyers evaluate businesses. They do not want to fall behind, but they are wary of wasting money, being misunderstood, or publishing content that makes their business look generic.
What they value most
Authenticity
The content should sound and feel like the real business.
Credibility
Their expertise must be represented accurately.
Control
They want approval and the ability to guide direction.
Ease
The process should reduce complexity, not create another job.
Consistency
They want a system that can keep moving even when the owner is busy.
Practicality
They care about business usefulness more than trends, virality, or novelty.

5. Master Empathy Map
The shared emotional and behavioral pattern across the ICP
This map summarizes what the broad Content Velocity buyer is likely to experience before they become a client. It is intentionally human rather than technical: the job of marketing is to show that we understand the tension they are living with.
THINKS“My business is better than it looks online.” “I should be more visible.” “I know this matters, but I do not know how to keep up.”
FEELSOverloaded, behind, cautious, protective of the brand, frustrated by inconsistency, and hopeful that a better system exists.
SEESCompetitors posting more often, fragmented channels, an outdated or thin digital footprint, and an endless stream of new tools and tactics.
HEARSThat AI is changing marketing, that content matters more, that search is shifting, and that every vendor has a different answer.
SAYS“I do not have time.” “I tried AI and it sounded generic.” “I know what should be done; it just never gets done.”
DOESRelies on referrals, posts inconsistently, starts and stops, delegates pieces, experiments with tools, or postpones content until there is time.
FEARSLooking fake, publishing inaccurate content, wasting money, losing control of the brand, buying another platform, or being disappointed again.
WANTSTo be findable, trusted, and consistent — with content that reflects real expertise while reducing the owner’s manual workload.
Marketing implication
The strongest external marketing makes the buyer feel understood before explaining the solution. Start with the gap between real-world credibility and online visibility, then show a path to consistent content that protects the buyer’s voice and keeps them in control.

6. Primary Persona — The Credible-but-Invisible Expert
The strongest core avatar
Who they are
An established expert, practitioner, consultant, specialist, advisor, professional service provider, or business owner with deep knowledge and years of credibility.
Business reality
They are respected in conversation, through referrals, in their profession, or in their local market. Their expertise lives in client work, explanations, stories, calls, presentations, and experience — but very little of it is visible online.
Primary tension
Their authority is trapped offline. Their digital presence makes them appear quieter, smaller, or less authoritative than they really are.
What they want
A digital footprint that reflects the expertise they have already earned: helpful articles, educational content, videos, social proof, and a coherent point of view.
What they fear
Looking generic, being misrepresented, sounding like AI, or investing in marketing that does not understand their business.
Action trigger
They realize referrals alone create a ceiling, prospects look them up before taking action, or competitors appear more credible simply because they publish more.
Marketing interpretation
Marketing should validate their existing credibility rather than imply they need to reinvent themselves. The promise is not “become an influencer.” It is “make the authority you already have visible.”
Empathy map
THINKS“I know my field. People would trust me if they could actually see what I know.”
FEELSProud of the business, slightly frustrated by the online gap, and cautious about outside marketers.
SEESCompetitors with weaker experience but stronger online presence.
HEARSThat visibility and AI matter, but the tools feel noisy and disconnected.
SAYS“I should be more visible.” “I do not have time to figure all of this out.”
DOESRelies on relationships and referrals; publishes inconsistently or not at all.
FEARSThat content will cheapen their reputation or sound unlike them.
WANTSTheir real-world authority to become easy to verify online.

7. Local Business / Local Authority Persona
The business that wants to become the obvious trusted expert in its market
Who they are
A local or regional operator, practice, specialty service, retail/service business, contractor, repair business, property-related company, or other locally chosen provider.
Business reality
Customers compare options, ask neighbors, read reviews, search online, and use visible activity as a shortcut for trust. The business may already have a solid reputation, but its content does not reinforce that reputation consistently.
Primary tension
Under-visibility in the market they already serve. Competitors may appear more active, current, or educational even when the business itself has stronger real-world experience.
What they want
Consistent local authority: useful explanations, market-specific education, visible expertise, and a digital presence that confirms the business is active and trustworthy.
What they fear
Being invisible when customers search, wasting money on broad generic marketing, or sounding like a national brand that does not understand the local customer.
Action trigger
A competitor begins showing up more, referrals soften, customers mention finding alternatives online, or the owner realizes the market does not know enough about what makes the business different.
Marketing interpretation
Use practical, market-specific language. Tie content to trust, education, and local decision-making. Avoid abstract AI positioning; the buyer needs to see how content helps people in their market recognize, understand, and choose them.
Empathy map
THINKS“We are good at what we do, but not enough people know it.”
FEELSCompetitive, busy, and frustrated that online visibility does not match local reputation.
SEESOther local businesses appearing more active and more current online.
HEARSCustomers say they found businesses through Google, social media, or referrals.
SAYS“I do not have time to post consistently.”
DOESRuns the business first; marketing happens when someone remembers.
FEARSLosing local mindshare to a less capable but more visible competitor.
WANTSTo become the business people think of first when the need arises.

8. Professional Services & High-Ticket B2B Persona
The buyer who does not need everyone — they need the right people to trust them
Who they are
A high-value consultant, advisor, recruiter, planner, specialist, B2B professional, legal/financial-adjacent expert, investor, or other service provider where one relationship can be economically meaningful.
Business reality
Their value is often obvious once a prospect is in a conversation, but it may be difficult to verify before the call. They may depend heavily on introductions and personal networks.
Primary tension
The business is too dependent on personal introductions and live explanations. Prospects do not see enough proof, education, or point of view before the sales conversation.
What they want
Authority assets that pre-educate and pre-build trust so qualified prospects understand the expertise before they book, reply, or accept an introduction.
What they fear
Looking lightweight, being compared only on price, or creating high-volume content that feels mass-market and undermines professional credibility.
Action trigger
The owner realizes referrals still lead to online research, wants warmer conversations, or sees that a single qualified client can justify sustained authority building.
Marketing interpretation
Lead with trust, authority, education, and quality of audience rather than raw traffic or volume. This persona responds to the idea that content should make the sales conversation easier before the conversation starts.
Empathy map
THINKS“I do not need everyone. I need the right people to understand why I am credible.”
FEELSConfident in the service, but aware the online proof is thinner than it should be.
SEESProspects researching before calls and competitors publishing thought leadership.
HEARSQuestions that could have been answered before a sales conversation.
SAYS“Referrals work, but they limit me.”
DOESNetworks, takes calls, explains the same concepts repeatedly, and underuses those explanations as content.
FEARSLow-quality visibility that attracts the wrong leads or weakens perceived expertise.
WANTSA smaller number of better-informed, higher-trust prospects.

9. Consultant / Coach / Expert Persona
The established advisor whose expertise is valuable but not consistently packaged as content
Who they are
An experienced consultant, coach, educator, trainer, advisor, strategist, or subject-matter expert with a proven offer and real client experience.
Business reality
They create value through ideas, frameworks, diagnosis, teaching, and personal insight. Much of the intellectual property is delivered verbally, repeatedly, and one client at a time.
Primary tension
The expertise is not systematically captured. The owner becomes the bottleneck because every article, video, post, and explanation starts with them.
What they want
A repeatable way to translate frameworks, FAQs, stories, opinions, and client lessons into content that educates the market and supports the offer.
What they fear
Generic “thought leadership,” shallow AI summaries, or content that loses nuance and makes the expert sound like everyone else.
Action trigger
They are tired of recreating explanations, want broader reach for a proven method, or realize their best thinking disappears when it is not captured.
Marketing interpretation
Respect the buyer’s expertise. Do not talk down to them or over-teach basic marketing. Show that the system organizes and multiplies what they already know while preserving nuance and point of view.
Empathy map
THINKS“I have more to say than I have time to publish.”
FEELSIntellectually confident but operationally overloaded.
SEESPeers building authority through consistent publishing.
HEARSClients asking the same core questions again and again.
SAYS“I know what I should be doing. I just do not have time.”
DOESCreates in bursts, then goes silent when client work gets busy.
FEARSLosing distinctiveness or publishing generic advice.
WANTSTheir thinking turned into a visible body of work without becoming a full-time creator.

What they fear

Use clear, reassuring, non-hype language. Emphasize accuracy, review, education, and trust. Content should reduce confusion and make expertise understandable without pretending content alone controls outcomes.

What they fear
Generic financial content, exaggerated promises, off-brand messaging, or anything that reduces a serious professional relationship to a marketing gimmick.
Action trigger
They recognize that prospects are researching more before calls, referrals need stronger digital reinforcement, or repeated client questions could become educational assets.
Marketing interpretation
Lead with credibility, clarity, and confidence. Emphasize that content can support trust and education before the conversation. Avoid mass-market hype, virality, and any implication of guaranteed business outcomes.

13. E-Commerce / Product-Brand Persona
The operator who needs more content without losing the brand
Who they are
A founder or operator of a real product business selling through a website, e-commerce platform, retail channel, marketplace, or multiple product lines.
Business reality
The products exist and may already sell, but the story, education, use cases, founder point of view, lifestyle context, and brand authority are not being developed consistently across channels.
Primary tension
Content is inconsistent, fragmented, or overly dependent on product pages and paid promotion. Agencies, freelancers, or AI tools may have misread the brand in the past.
What they want
More consistent storytelling and education around products, use cases, customer problems, founder story, and brand point of view — without sacrificing visual or verbal consistency.
What they fear

Action trigger

Marketing interpretation
For this persona, brand control is central. Show that content can expand while voice, visual direction, and approval remain protected. Avoid language that makes content sound like a commodity or a volume game.

16. Agency-Burned Brand Protector Persona
The qualified buyer who wants help but is afraid of being misunderstood again
Who they are
An established owner who has previously hired agencies, freelancers, marketers, content writers, social managers, SEO providers, or AI tools and felt disappointed by the result.
Business reality
The issue was not always “nothing got delivered.” Often the deeper problem was misrepresentation: the work sounded wrong, looked wrong, missed nuance, required too much rewriting, or created more management instead of relief.
Primary tension
They want help but have learned not to trust promises easily. Their skepticism is often a sign that they care deeply about the quality of how the business is represented.
What they want
A process that proves understanding before scaling: voice capture, early examples, clear review, visible quality control, and a collaborative implementation period.
What they fear
Paying again for generic work, losing control, being told to “trust the process” without proof, or discovering that the owner still has to rewrite everything.
Action trigger
They see evidence that the process starts by understanding the business, notice there is a review loop, or believe the system will reduce rather than increase their workload.
Marketing interpretation
Do not dismiss prior bad experiences. Acknowledge the concern through process clarity. This buyer needs to see that the business is the source material and that review, approval, and feedback are built into the relationship.
Empathy map
THINKS“Nobody really understands my business.”
FEELSCautious, protective, and willing to listen if the process feels credible.
SEESA history of vendors promising simplicity but creating rework.
HEARSMarketing claims that sound too broad or too good to be true.

DOESReviews closely, asks practical questions, and looks for proof before committing.

17. What They Are Trying to Escape
The problems beneath “we need more content”
The ICP is usually trying to escape several overlapping situations. Marketing works best when it names the lived problem, not just the desired deliverable.
Manual content exhaustion
They have tried writing posts, recording videos, building calendars, using prompts, delegating, or hiring help. Consistency breaks because production does not fit naturally into the operating rhythm of the business.
Digital invisibility
They may be respected offline while appearing thin, outdated, or quiet online. Prospects cannot easily verify what makes them credible.
Agency disappointment
Prior help may have delivered assets but still missed the brand, tone, nuance, or point — leaving the owner to rewrite and re-manage.
AI frustration
They have tried AI and disliked the output, or they are interested but intimidated. The common concern is not “AI cannot write.” It is “AI does not sound like me.”
Referral ceiling
The business grows when people already know or recommend it, but the owner wants a stronger way to reach beyond the existing network.
Owner bottleneck
Even when other people help, the owner is still the person every content decision, idea, draft, and approval depends on.
Important distinction
Their core problem is rarely a lack of value. It is the inability to turn existing value into consistent, visible, trustworthy content without creating another full-time job.

18. What They Ultimately Want
The transformation they are actually buying
The buyer is not simply shopping for “AI content.” At a deeper level, they want relief from inconsistency, invisibility, and execution overload while protecting the trust they have already earned.
Desired outcomes
More consistent visibility without doing everything manually.
A stronger online body of evidence that reflects the quality of the real business.
Content that sounds natural, accurate, and recognizably theirs.
A practical way to turn expertise, stories, customer questions, products, and point of view into reusable content.

The emotional outcome
They want the business to feel “caught up” and represented properly online. They want to stop carrying the nagging sense that the expertise is real but invisible. The ideal future state is not fame — it is being consistently findable, trusted, current, and credible.

19. Their Relationship With AI
AI is a context, not the identity of the buyer
The ideal customer is not usually an AI power user. Their relationship with AI falls into a small number of practical patterns, and each requires a different explanation.
AI-naive but interested
They know AI matters but do not know how to use it strategically. Translate technology into business outcomes and reduce intimidation.
AI-frustrated
They tried ChatGPT or another tool and disliked generic, robotic, or off-brand output. Show why voice, context, and process matter.
Active but overwhelmed
They already use content and AI tools but cannot keep up. Emphasize leverage, workflow, and removing the owner as the bottleneck.
Digitally underdeveloped
They have not done much content yet. Connect visibility to credibility, referrals, and being researched before the sale.
Marketing-literate and time-poor
They understand the concepts and may already know the tools. They only fit if they value implementation rather than cheap self-serve software.
What marketing should not assume
Interest in AI does not automatically mean strong fit.
Technology sophistication does not automatically mean higher buying intent.
A buyer can be skeptical of AI and still be highly qualified.
The core promise should not be “more AI.” The buyer is trying to solve visibility, consistency, authenticity, and execution.
Public positioning rule
AI should be presented as the leverage inside the content system, not the hero of the story. The hero is the business owner’s expertise becoming easier to see, understand, and trust.

20. Buyer Awareness Stages
How the same ICP sounds different depending on what they have already tried
The public marketing partner should recognize that a strong-fit buyer can enter from different starting points. The core ICP stays the same; the message changes based on what the person already understands.
Stage 1 — New to AI/content systems
They know they need more visibility but feel unsure where to start. They need education, reassurance, and a guided path.
Stage 2 — Tried AI and disliked it
They already understand the promise of AI but have personally experienced generic output. They need proof of specificity, voice, and quality control.
Stage 3 — Already creating but overwhelmed
They believe in content and may already publish. Their issue is bandwidth and inconsistency. They need execution relief and systemization.
Stage 4 — Credible but digitally quiet
They have built the business through referrals, relationships, or reputation. They need to understand the risk of a weak digital footprint and the value of visible authority.
Stage 5 — Marketing-literate split
Some are excellent fits because they are time-poor and want implementation. Others are poor fits because they mainly want cheap software or another tool.
Messaging progression
Start with the business problem they already feel.
Explain why random or generic content does not solve it.
Show the role of brand voice, expertise, and market context.
Reduce fear by explaining review, approval, and guided implementation.
Only then explain the content formats and production system.

21. Primary Buying Fears & Objections
What can stop a good-fit buyer from moving forward
Most objections come from a handful of understandable fears. These should be treated as information about the buyer’s risk perception, not as something to “overcome” aggressively.
“Will this sound like me?”
Fear of generic, robotic, or off-brand content. Reassure with business context, voice capture, feedback, and review.
“I am not technical.”
Fear of buying another platform and getting stuck. Reassure with guided implementation and a simple ongoing role.
“I have been burned before.”
Fear of another vendor misunderstanding the business. Reassure with early examples, transparent process, and quality control.
“I do not have time.”
Fear that the system becomes another task. Show how the owner stays the expert and approver rather than full-time producer.
“I need to fix my website first.”
They may believe content cannot start until everything else is perfect. Explain that voice, messaging, and content direction can support the broader digital presence.
“How does this get customers?”
They want a business connection, not vanity metrics. Show how content supports findability, trust, education, and the next step while avoiding guarantees.
“What if the content is wrong?”
Fear of inaccuracy or misrepresentation. Emphasize expert review and approval.
“What happens after implementation?”
Fear of dependence or abandonment. Clarify that the goal is a repeatable system and a clear ongoing path.
Tone rule
The right posture is consultative, clear, and credible. Acknowledge the concern, explain the process that reduces the risk, and avoid exaggerated certainty.

22. What Builds Trust
The proof and reassurance this ICP needs
For this audience, process proof can matter as much as performance proof. They want evidence that the system respects their expertise, understands their business, and will not remove human judgment from important decisions.
Trust builders
A clear explanation of how the business is understood before content production begins.
Evidence that voice, positioning, offers, and customer context guide the output.
Early examples or samples that let the buyer assess fit before a large volume is produced.
A visible review and approval process.
A guided implementation period rather than a blank self-serve platform.
Realistic expectations about what content can and cannot control.
Examples showing articles, social content, video, and other assets working together as a system.
Established business owners or peers who can speak credibly about ease, accuracy, and the implementation experience.
Plain language that does not hide behind AI jargon or marketing hype.
What erodes trust
“Push-button” or “set it and forget it” language.
Claims that remove the buyer from review or imply AI knows the business automatically.
Overpromising leads, rankings, conversions, or virality.
Leading with raw content volume before explaining quality and control.
Futuristic, gimmicky, or overly polished creative that makes the offer feel like generic tech marketing.

24. Creative & Communication Style
How the marketing should feel
Creative should be authenticity-first: real, direct, grounded, and businesslike. The audience is often skeptical, time-poor, established, and protective of reputation. Highly polished “AI company” advertising can make the offer feel less trustworthy rather than more sophisticated.
Overall feel
A trusted business owner or strategist speaking plainly to another business owner.
Best visual context
Real people, natural lighting, office or business environments, simple screen walkthroughs, and clear examples of content.
Best proof
Voice capture, review process, content examples, implementation support, and established owners using the system.
Best story frame
A person hears about the business, looks it up, and either finds proof of expertise or finds too little to build confidence.
Best emotional tone
Direct, honest, reassuring, practical, experienced, and slightly urgent without fearmongering.
Avoid
Robots, neon AI graphics, “glowing brain” visuals, overdesigned agency ads, generic stock business footage, hype reels, or lifestyle flexing.
Creative themes to explore
The look-up moment: what does a referral or prospect find when they research the business?
Content that sounds like the real owner, not a blank AI prompt.
The difference between random posting and a repeatable content engine.
The owner bottleneck: content should not stop because the owner had a busy week.
Offline credibility versus online silence.
Guided implementation: the buyer is not left alone with another platform.
Creative should make the viewer think:
“They understand the problem. This does not sound like cheap AI. I can stay in control, and I may finally get content off my plate.”

26. Marketing Team Quick Reference
The one-page operating summary for external agencies and creative partners
WHO
Established owners and expert-led brands with real business substance but inconsistent or underdeveloped online visibility.
CORE PROBLEM
Their real-world credibility is stronger than their digital presence, and the owner does not have time to manually fix the gap.
CORE DESIRE
Consistent, authentic, useful content that turns existing expertise into visible authority without making the owner a full-time creator.
EMOTIONAL STATE
Busy, frustrated by inconsistency, cautious about AI and agencies, protective of reputation, and hopeful for a simpler system.
PRIMARY FEARS
Generic content, misrepresentation, platform overwhelm, wasted money, loss of control, and another disappointing vendor experience.
TRUST BUILDERS
Voice and context first, clear review/approval, early examples, guided implementation, realistic expectations, and visible process.
BEST MESSAGE
Your expertise is already real. The opportunity is to make it consistently visible online while you stay the expert and approver.
BEST CREATIVE
Real people, plain business language, direct-to-camera explanation, credible examples, simple walkthroughs, and relatable business scenarios.
DO NOT LEAD WITH
AI hype, raw content volume, virality, unlimited automation, cheap content, guaranteed leads, or complicated platform features.
BEST-FIT SIGNAL
Operating business + decision authority + visibility pain + limited time + willingness to collaborate and delegate execution.
Final principle
Content Velocity marketing should never make the buyer feel that AI is replacing their expertise. The most resonant story is that the business already has authority, experience, products, stories, answers, and credibility — and the content system helps that value show up more consistently where customers are looking.

B. External Agency Briefing Checklist
Questions every marketing partner should be able to answer before creating a campaign
Before writing ads, landing pages, emails, webinars, or creative concepts, confirm that the work can answer these questions from the buyer’s point of view.
Customer recognition
Can the prospect immediately tell that the message is for an established owner or expert with a real business — not a startup dreamer or AI hobbyist?
Pain clarity
Does the message name the visibility, consistency, time, or credibility gap before talking about the product?
Persona fit
Which persona is the campaign speaking to, and what professional context makes that pain specific?
Authenticity
Does the creative reassure the buyer that their voice, expertise, and brand matter?
Implementation
Is it clear that the buyer is not simply being handed another tool to figure out alone?
Control
Does the buyer understand that they remain the expert and approver?
Business outcome
Does the content connect to being easier to find, understand, trust, and choose rather than vanity metrics alone?
AI framing
Is AI positioned as leverage inside the system rather than the main reason to buy?
Trust
Is the tone credible, grounded, direct, and free of exaggerated promises?
Expectation setting
Does the campaign avoid implying guaranteed leads, rankings, virality, or fully automated marketing?
Creative style
Would an experienced business owner see the creative as professional and relatable rather than gimmicky or tech-bro?
Fit boundary
Is the campaign likely to attract operating businesses with real authority rather than bargain tool shoppers, passive-income seekers, or influencer audiences?
If a campaign cannot answer these questions clearly, return to the master ICP before producing more creative. The goal is not simply to generate attention; it is to attract the established, credibility-rich, time-poor buyer Content Velocity is designed to serve.
