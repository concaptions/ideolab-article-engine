# How these articles should sound

David's feedback on the first two drafts, 22 September 2026: the reviews article was good, the agency article was a good concept with poor execution, and the writing was "not in a conversational tone that is direct response marketing." He also said a direct response writer would never invent a phrase like "context tax", and that there are a hundred better ways to say the same thing that are easier and more fun to read and create more curiosity.

There is still no brand voice document. David will write one after he reads eight sample articles. Until then, this file is the tone rule, and it outranks anything in the older instructions.

## The one test

Read the paragraph out loud. If it sounds like a person talking to one other person across a table, keep it. If it sounds like a consultant presenting, rewrite it.

## Rules

**Write to one person.** You, not businesses, not operators, not owners. "Your best client" beats "the typical client relationship."

**Never invent a term.** No coined phrases, no capitalised concepts of our own making, nothing the reader has to learn before the article works. If an idea needs a name, use words the reader already owns. The failure that started this: "the context tax" should have been "the hours you never bill for."

**Open with a moment or a claim, not homework.** The first line should make the reader want the second line. A scene they recognise, a number that stings, a sentence that contradicts what they believe. Never open by asking the reader to calculate something or by explaining what the article will cover.

**Short sentences, mixed with longer ones.** A run of five short sentences is as tiring as a run of five long ones. Vary it. Fragments are allowed.

**Concrete beats abstract every time.** Not "inconsistent marketing output." Instead: "you posted three times in March and nothing since." Name the plumber, the dentist, the Tuesday, the £3,000 retainer.

**Curiosity between sections.** End a section with something that makes the next heading worth reading. Subheads should promise something, not label a topic. "Where the money actually leaks" beats "Cost analysis."

**Say the benefit plainly.** Direct response never makes the reader work out why they should care. Tell them, in their words, and then show it.

**Cut the throat-clearing.** No "it is worth noting", "in today's landscape", "the reality is". Start at the point.

**Contractions are fine.** You'll, don't, it's. That is how people talk.

**Questions the reader answers in their head.** One or two per article, not more. "When did you last look at your own Google listing?"

**No hedging stacks.** One qualifier is honest. Three is cowardice.

## What does not change

Everything in `brand-brief.md` still applies: the claim rules, the banned phrases, the IdeoLab vocabulary, no invented proof, real citations only, one capability per article, product mentioned only after the reader recognises the problem. Direct response is about how it reads, not a licence to overclaim. David's ICP is explicit that hype and false autonomy are the fastest way to lose both audiences.

## Two openings, before and after

Before, from the agency draft David rejected:

> Run one number before you read anything else. Take a typical monthly retainer and divide it by the hours your team truly spends on that client, including the hours nobody logs.

After:

> Your best client pays you three grand a month. Do you know what they actually cost you? Not the work you quoted. The other stuff.

Same point. The second one does not ask the reader to do arithmetic before they have any reason to care.

## Format, per David

Each article states its main keyword at the top, in the frontmatter as `main_keyword` and in the review copy so he can see it at a glance.
