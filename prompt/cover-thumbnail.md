# Cover thumbnail

How the cover image is made from 20 October 2026. scripts/engine/thumbnail.mjs
reads this file, gives the section for the site to the writer model with the
finished article, and the model returns a headline and an image prompt in the
same shape as the example below. The examples are the two covers David approved
on 20 October ("YES sooo much better"). They show the standard; their scenes are
not to be reused.

These are not David's thirty style prompt followed to the letter. That prompt
produced the first two covers, and he called them terrible. What he approved
came from prompts written like the ones here, so these are the standard.

## Rules for both sites

- Text on the image, on both sites. A headline of three to five words, the
  reader's benefit or the article's sharpest idea. Never the post title and
  never the keyword repeated: his own sample covers printed the title in big
  letters, and a cover laid out like his sample looks copied from it.
- One concrete visual idea from the article. What kind of scene depends on the
  site, see its section: IdeoLab uses objects and metaphors, Strategic Marketer
  uses a real moment with a person in it. Choose the composition each time. Not
  a laptop or a browser window by default, never a robot, a lightbulb or a
  dashboard of numbers.
- The headline is the only readable text. Every prompt ends by saying so, with
  the headline spelled out, and the engine reads the image back to check it.

## Scene rules

Added 22 October 2026, after David said the covers were "still terrible". Three
of the four Strategic Marketer covers of 20 and 21 October earned it. The style
was right; the scenes were not. What went wrong in each, so it is not repeated:

- "Save Time Before Spending": a basket of boxes with two wrenches, a curved
  track of cards, a brass gate, a tray and a stopwatch. Five ideas, none of them
  readable at a glance.
- "Save Time, Not Guesswork": the article said "buy more tools" and the cover
  showed a hammer, pliers and a wrench. Software tools are never drawn as hand
  tools.
- "Earn Buyer Trust": a row of tiles ending in a barred gate that reads as a jail
  door, on a background split down the right side by a visible seam.

So every scene now has to pass all of these:

- One hero doing one thing: one object or one tight group on IdeoLab, one
  person (two at most) in one moment on Strategic Marketer. At most three other
  small supporting pieces. If the scene needs a sentence with "and" to explain
  it, it is two scenes; keep the stronger one.
- A viewer understands what it says in two seconds, at the size of a phone card.
  The approved two do: gears driving bars up, a pencil line becoming a website.
- Nothing literal from a figure of speech: no hand tools for "tools", no baskets
  or carts for "buying", no keys and locks for "unlock", no puzzles, no
  rockets, no stopwatches or hourglasses for "time".
- Nothing that reads as negative or odd: no bars, cages, gates, chains, broken
  or melting things, nothing that looks like a prison, a trap or a warning.
- One continuous background, no panels, seams or split tones.
- The hook and the scene say the same thing. Write that one sentence first; if
  the picture does not show it, change the picture.
- Do not repeat a scene or a hook already used on this site.

## Site: ideolab

Style: bright, airy pastel SaaS 2.5D illustration, soft and premium. Rounded
floating objects and simple interface cards with soft shadows, luminous pastel
gradients (peach, baby blue, lavender, mint, white) with one clear medium blue
accent. Headline in a large bold rounded sans serif, deep navy. Objects and
interface pieces only, no people and no faces.

Approved example, "AI Text to Website Builder" article, headline "Your Website,
No Designer Needed":

A 16:9 blog thumbnail in a bright, airy pastel SaaS 2.5D illustration style. Right half: a large bold rounded sans-serif headline in deep navy on two lines, reading exactly "Your Website," on the first line and "No Designer Needed" on the second line, right of center with generous margins from every edge. Left half: a friendly oversized pastel yellow pencil leans diagonally and writes one soft wavy line on a small sheet of paper; the wavy line lifts off the paper as a glowing lavender ribbon that curls upward and folds itself into a small three-dimensional website standing like a tiny building, its floors made of stacked pastel website sections: a header bar, a picture block with a soft sun, a rounded blue button and a row of cards. From the roof of that little website building a white paper plane is taking off with a soft trail, as if the site is launching. Background: a soft gradient from peach to baby blue with a few blurred rounded shapes and small sparkles, gentle soft shadows. Palette: peach, baby blue, lavender, mint, white, with one clear medium blue accent. The only readable text anywhere in the image is the two-line headline, spelled exactly as given. No other words, letters, numbers, logos or watermarks, and nothing readable on the paper, the website or any other surface.

## Site: strategicmarketer

Changed 22 October 2026. The covers made from 20 October were still lifes of
cards, trays, gears and brass rails on ivory. They looked premium and told
no story: without the headline nobody could tell what any of them was about.
David's own ICP for this site (knowledge/strategicmarketer/
icp-partner-edition.txt, section 24) already says what this reader responds to:

- Best visual context: "Real people, natural lighting, office or business
  environments, simple screen walkthroughs, and clear examples of content."
- Best story frame: "A person hears about the business, looks it up, and either
  finds proof of expertise or finds too little to build confidence."
- Themes: the look-up moment, content that sounds like the real owner, random
  posting against a repeatable engine, the owner as the bottleneck, offline
  credibility against online silence, help to put it in place.
- Avoid: "Robots, neon AI graphics, glowing brain visuals, overdesigned agency
  ads, generic stock business footage, hype reels, or lifestyle flexing."

So every Strategic Marketer cover is a small story: one real moment from the
reader's working week, photographed.

The story comes first, in "says", written as who does what and why it matters:
"A customer who looked a bakery up walks in with the phone still in hand and
the owner greets them, because what they found online brought them to the
door."

- Who: the owner of an established business, and often the customer too. Take
  the business from the article's own examples or the one the engine names, and
  vary it from cover to cover.
- The moment: the one moment the article is about, usually the relief once it
  is solved, because the headline names a result. The owner back with a client
  instead of at the desk. A customer walking in who found the business online.
  A new hire answering a question alone. The owner leaving in daylight.
- The story is told by people and things in the room, never by a screen.
  Nothing on a screen can be read, so a screen cannot carry the story. A phone
  or tablet can be in the picture as a supporting prop, but the main action is
  never someone looking at or tapping a device. Ask: with every screen blank,
  would a stranger still get it?
- The strongest covers have two people and the moment between them that the
  article makes possible: welcomed, answered, served, agreed, handed over.
  Otherwise one person with a result you can see.
- Readable at phone size: one action a stranger can name in a sentence. Only
  real objects a real business has; no invented gadgets, discs, buttons or
  devices.

How it looks:

- One continuous editorial photograph, edge to edge, in natural window light,
  a real business interior, calm and uncluttered, colour graded to the house
  palette: warm ivory and sand, sage teal, slate blue, small touches of brushed
  brass.
- The headline is set over a calm, softly out of focus part of that same scene
  (a wall, a window, an empty stretch of counter) in an elegant high contrast
  serif, deep charcoal navy (ivory if that part is dark), with generous
  margins. Never describe the image as a left part and a right part or give the
  headline its own panel: on 22 October that made the image model put two
  photos side by side with a hard seam between them, twice in three tries.
- Not stock: nobody posing, nobody looking at the camera, no forced smiles, no
  handshakes, no group around a laptop, no one pointing at a chart.
- One or two people, caught mid action. Natural hands and faces. Vary who they
  are from cover to cover: age, gender, build, clothes, and the shot (close,
  wide, from behind, through a doorway). The first two story covers were both a
  woman in her forties in a cardigan at a reception desk with a tablet; that
  sameness is the thing to avoid. Vary the action too: handing over a paper
  and two people across a table are the easy defaults, and they wear out fast.
  Fitting, serving, showing, walking a customer through the space, packing an
  order, opening up in the morning, finishing a job on site.
- Nothing readable on paper, signs, packaging, mugs, folders or clothing either.
  A prop that invites a label (a "tax" folder, a menu, a shop sign) is left out
  or shown blank.
- No robots, glowing brains, neon, holograms, floating icons, arrows or charts.
  Nothing in the picture says "AI".

The palette and serif David approved on 20 October stay. The gears still life
("Make Every Marketing Dollar Work") is the record of them, not the model for
new scenes.

Example, written 22 October for "Is Your Online Reputation Costing You
Sales?", headline "From Search To Your Door":

A 16:9 blog thumbnail, one continuous editorial photograph running edge to edge, in soft natural morning window light, calm and uncluttered, colour graded to warm ivory, sand, sage teal and slate blue with small touches of brushed brass. Inside a small neighbourhood bakery, seen from behind the counter at eye level: a customer in a slate blue coat has just stepped in through the glass door, a phone still in one hand at their side, and the owner, a man in his fifties in a sage apron, looks up from arranging loaves and smiles in welcome. The moment says: they looked the bakery up, liked what they found, and came in. A few loaves on a wooden shelf and a small brass bell above the door are the only props. The two people stand toward the left of the frame; toward the right, the same room continues into a calm ivory wall softly out of focus with morning light on it, and the headline is set on that wall in an elegant high-contrast serif in deep charcoal navy on two lines, reading exactly "From Search" on the first line and "To Your Door" on the second line, left aligned, with generous margins from every edge. One photograph, no panels, borders or split. Natural faces and hands, nobody looking at the camera, no posing. The only readable text in the entire image is that headline, spelled exactly as given. No other words, letters, numbers, labels, logos or watermarks anywhere, including on the phone, the door, the shelves, packaging and clothing.
