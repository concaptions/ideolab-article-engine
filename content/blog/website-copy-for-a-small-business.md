A joinery business in Leeds has one hundred and forty one words above the fold on its homepage. Counting them takes a minute and is worth doing to your own.

Of those one hundred and forty one, not one is the word wardrobe, or staircase, or Leeds. Three are the business name. Eleven are navigation. The rest describe a commitment to quality and a passion for craftsmanship.

A stranger arriving on that page is trying to answer three questions. Is this the right kind of business for my problem. Do they work where I am. What does it cost, roughly. The page answers the first somewhere below the fold, the second in the footer, and the third nowhere at all.

Good website copy for a small business is not a writing exercise. It is putting the three answers in the order they are being asked, in the words the person is already using, and then getting out of the way.

## What a visitor is actually doing

Nobody reads a website. They scan it for evidence that it applies to them, and they leave the moment they stop finding any.

A joinery business with a page headed "Craftsmanship, Refined" is asking a visitor to work for that evidence. A page headed "Fitted wardrobes and staircases, built in our Leeds workshop" hands it over in eight words. Same business, same quality of work, completely different amount of effort demanded from a stranger.

The refined version usually exists because somebody was worried about sounding ordinary. Ordinary is what gets read.

> [!tip]
> Take the first sentence of your homepage and ask whether a competitor two towns away could use it unchanged. If they could, it is not describing your business. It is describing your industry.

## Write the three answers first

Before any styling, write the three answers in plain sentences and put them where a scan will catch them.

What you do, specifically enough to rule yourself out. "Bookkeeping for tradespeople and small builders" is more useful than "financial services for growing businesses", because ruling out the wrong visitor is as valuable as attracting the right one.

Where you work, in the words locals use. Named towns, a radius, the areas you do not cover. This one sentence resolves more bounces than any design change.

What it costs, at least approximately. A starting point, a typical range, or an honest explanation of what moves the number. A page with no price signal sends the visitor to a competitor to find one, and they often stay there.

The usual objection is that every job is different and a number would be misleading. Both things are true and neither stops you writing "most fitted wardrobes we build come in between two and four thousand, depending on the timber and whether the alcove is square". That sentence loses the enquiries that were never going to buy and keeps the ones that were, which is the trade every small business says it wants and then avoids making.

## The fourth answer, which is what happens next

Three answers get somebody interested. The fourth gets them to act, and it is usually the weakest line on the page.

"Get in touch" is not an instruction. It leaves the visitor to decide how, and deciding is friction. Say what happens: a phone number with the hours it is answered, or a form that asks for three things rather than eight, followed by a sentence saying when somebody replies.

Length matters here more than wording. A form asking for name, postcode and a short description of the job gets filled in. The same form with budget, timescale, how did you hear about us, and a paragraph box gets abandoned halfway, and nobody who abandoned it will tell you.

Put a photograph of real work next to it. Not a stock image of a smiling advisor, and not the logo. The finished job, taken on a phone, in the place it was installed.

## Use their words rather than yours

The phrases that work are not invented at a desk. They are already written down, in enquiry emails, in what people say on the phone, and in reviews.

Reading reviews for language is a method of its own, set out in [how to use customer reviews in marketing](/blog/how-to-use-customer-reviews-in-marketing). The short version for a website is that the phrase appearing in three reviews belongs on the page, and it usually belongs in a heading.

A domestic electrician found that customers kept using the word "tidy". Not qualified, not certified, not reliable. Tidy. It went into a subheading, along with two photographs of a finished consumer unit and a swept floor, and the enquiry form stopped asking people to describe their job in a paragraph.

The word had been sitting in his reviews for four years. It never occurred to him to use it because it sounded too small to put on a website, which is exactly why it worked. Nobody else in his trade was saying it, and everybody hiring an electrician for the first time was quietly worried about it.

That is the general shape of the exercise. The phrase that feels too ordinary to publish is usually the one doing the deciding, and the phrase that feels professional enough to lead with is usually the one every competitor has already used.

## Make it readable before you make it beautiful

Copy that cannot be read comfortably is not copy.

The W3C's [contrast minimum](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html) requires that normal text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 against its background, and large text at least 3 to 1, with large text defined as at least 18 point, or 14 point bold. Pale grey on white, which appears on a large share of small business sites because it looked calm in the design file, frequently fails that.

> [!warning]
> Light grey body text is the most common accessibility failure on a small business site and also the most common reason older customers give up on a page. For a trade whose buyers are over fifty, it is a commercial problem before it is a compliance one.

## What search actually asks of you

There is a persistent belief that a small business site needs technical work before it can be found. Usually it does not.

Google's [SEO starter guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) states that the vast majority of sites listed in its results are found and added automatically as it crawls the web, and that search engines will likely understand your pages as they are right now, regardless of how your site is organised. It also notes that headings being out of order does not matter from a search perspective.

Which puts the work back where it belongs. Not on structure tricks, but on whether the page says something specific enough to be worth finding. The three answers, in the customer's words, on a page that can be read.

## The ratio worth counting

Forget time on page and bounce rate for a quarter. Count one thing: how many enquiries arrive already knowing what they want.

An enquiry that opens with "I need a fitted wardrobe for an alcove, we are in Horsforth, is two thousand realistic" is a page that did its job. An enquiry that opens with "hi, do you do kitchens" is a page that made somebody ask a question it should have answered.

Track the ratio for eight weeks. When the first kind starts outnumbering the second, the copy is working, and the phone conversations get shorter and better at the same time.
