"We partnered with the client to deliver a bespoke, data-driven solution that exceeded expectations across all key metrics."

That sentence, in one arrangement or another, sits on the case study pages of four different agencies in the same city. It names nothing. It could be lifted from any of the four and dropped into the other three, and no reader would notice.

The page it belongs to runs to nine hundred words without saying what the client sold, what was broken when the work started, what was tried first, or what any of it cost.

Most guidance on how to write a case study is about structure, and structure is the easy part. The hard part is that a case study is a claim about your competence, made to a buyer who has already been disappointed by somebody with a similar page.

## The question it has to answer

A prospect reading a case study is not asking whether the work went well. Nobody publishes the ones that did not.

They are asking a narrower question. Has this agency handled a situation like mine, and can they describe it precisely enough that I believe they were actually there.

Precision is the whole mechanism. "Increased traffic significantly" is a claim anyone can make. "Organic traffic to the twelve service pages went from 1,900 to 4,300 sessions a month over five months, and enquiries from those pages went from four to eleven" is a claim only somebody who did the work can make.

> [!tip]
> Before writing, find the one sentence in the project that would have been impossible to write in advance. The unexpected finding, the thing that broke, the number nobody predicted. Build the case study around that sentence.

## Settle the numbers before you write

Ask permission first, because rewriting a finished case study around a redaction is miserable work.

Go to the client with three specific questions. Can we use the company name. Can we use the figures as they are, as percentages, or not at all. Is there anything commercially sensitive in the timeline. Most clients say yes to more than agencies assume, and the ones who say no usually have a reason worth knowing.

Then be careful about how a strong result is presented. The FTC's [endorsement guides](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking) state that an endorsement must reflect the honest opinion of the endorser and cannot be used to make a claim the marketer could not legally make. They also say that where an advertiser lacks proof the endorser's experience represents what people generally achieve, the ad must make clear what the generally expected results are.

> [!warning]
> The best result you ever produced is the most dangerous one to lead with. Presented as what clients get, it becomes a claim about typical outcomes. Presented as what happened on this account, in these conditions, it stays true and reads as more credible anyway.

## The shape that holds attention

Six parts, in this order, and none of them longer than they need to be.

The situation. Who the client was and what kind of business, in enough detail that a similar buyer recognises themselves. The problem, stated as the client stated it rather than as you diagnosed it. What was tried first, including the approach that was dropped. What was actually done. What changed, with figures and a time period attached. And what would be done differently, which is the part that makes the other five believable.

That last one is where most agencies stop short. A case study with no friction in it reads like a brochure, because a project with no friction in it has never happened.

| Section | What it is doing | The failure to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The situation | Letting a similar buyer recognise themselves | Describing the client as "a leading provider" |
| The problem | Stating it in the client's language | Stating your diagnosis instead |
| What was tried first | Showing the work was thought about | Skipping straight to the thing that worked |
| What was done | The specifics somebody could argue with | A list of services with no sequence |
| What changed | Figures with a time period attached | A percentage with no baseline |
| What would change | Making the other five believable | Leaving it out to look flawless |

## Fifteen minutes on the phone

The strongest material in a case study usually comes from a short call with the client after the work has landed, and almost nobody makes it.

Four questions is enough. What were you worried about before we started. What did you expect to happen that did not. What changed internally that we would not have seen. What would you tell somebody considering this.

Record it with permission and use the sentences as they come out. The answer to the second question is frequently the case study's opening line, because it is the thing neither side predicted, and unpredicted things are the ones a reader believes.

## Write it in the client's words

The language of a case study usually comes from the agency, which is why so many of them sound the same.

Better material exists. The client's own emails at the start of the engagement, the way they described the problem on the first call, what their customers wrote afterwards. Mining that source is worth doing on its own terms, which is the argument in [how to use customer reviews in marketing](/blog/how-to-use-customer-reviews-in-marketing), and it applies exactly as well to proof as it does to copy.

One agency published a case study whose headline was a sentence from the client's first email: "we are invisible outside a five mile radius." It did more than any version the agency wrote, because a prospect with the same problem recognised the wording before they had read a single figure.

## The one page on your site that is genuinely original

Case studies are one of the few pages on an agency site with genuinely original information, and that matters.

Google's guidance on [creating helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) asks whether content clearly demonstrates first-hand expertise and a depth of knowledge, for example expertise that comes from having actually used a product or service, and whether it provides original information, reporting, research or analysis. It also asks whether it is self-evident to visitors who authored the content.

A real project write-up with named people, real figures and a date on it satisfies all three without any additional effort. Which is a rare position to be in, and most agencies waste it by publishing the bespoke, data-driven version instead.

## Where the material already is

The reason case studies get written badly is almost never that nobody can write. It is that they are written six months late, from memory, by whoever has a free afternoon.

The material was all there at the time. The research done before the pitch, the client's documents, the first brief, the figures that were pulled every month. It ended up in four places and by the time somebody needs it for a case study, two of those places belong to a person who has left.

## Back to that sentence

The four agencies with the interchangeable sentence are not badly run. Three of them do good work. They published a page that proves nothing because it was written at the end, in a hurry, by someone protecting themselves from saying anything a client might object to.

The version that wins is duller to write and far more specific. It names the trade, the town, the starting figure and the thing that went wrong in week three. It gets approved by the client because they were asked in advance. And it can only have been written by the people who were there.

That is the whole difference, and it is not a writing problem.
