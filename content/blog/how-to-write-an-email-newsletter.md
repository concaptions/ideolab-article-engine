The most common reason a newsletter fails is not that it is badly written. It is that it was never for anybody.

A monthly round-up of what a business has been up to has no reader. The customers do not care, the owner knows it, and the send date moves quietly backwards until it stops. Nobody cancels a newsletter. They just skip a month, then a quarter.

The newsletters that survive are narrow and slightly self-interested to read. They tell somebody something they can use before the end of the week, and that is the whole test. Everything about how to write an email newsletter follows from deciding what the reader is getting, and the answer cannot be "an update".

## Pick what the reader is getting

A small commercial cleaning company sends one email a month with a single subject: something that had gone wrong in a building that month, and what it cost to fix.

Blocked gutters after a storm. A fire door propped open during a heatwave and what the inspection said about it. A carpet ruined by the wrong cleaner in a reception area. No offers, no news, roughly three hundred words.

Their facilities manager readers open it because it might be their building next month. That is a reason, and reasons are the scarce part.

The company did not decide on that format. It arrived by accident, when an owner emailed nine clients about a burst pipe he had found on a Sunday, because he thought two of them had the same pipework. Four of the nine replied. One asked him to come and check.

Nothing about that email was clever. It was useful to a specific person with a specific building, and it was sent by somebody who had actually been there on the Sunday. Every month since has been built on the same test, which is whether the thing being described would have been worth a phone call.

> [!tip]
> Write the one line answer to "why would somebody open this" before writing anything else. If the answer is "to find out what we have been doing", the newsletter has no reader yet and the writing will not fix it.

## Three parts, and always the same three

Three parts is usually enough, and keeping the same three trains people to know where they are.

One thing worth knowing, which is the reason the email exists. One thing to do about it, short and specific. And one line about what is coming next, which gives the following send a reason to exist as well.

Company news, if it goes in at all, goes at the bottom in two sentences. A new hire is genuinely interesting to about four people on the list, and putting it at the top costs you the other four hundred.

Subject lines follow the same logic. Describe what is inside rather than performing at the reader. "The fire door thing that cost a client £2,400" is opened. "Your monthly update from the team" is not, and both take the same time to write.

| Part | What it is doing | How long |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One thing worth knowing | Giving the reader a reason to have opened it | Two or three short paragraphs |
| One thing to do | Turning interest into something actionable | Two or three lines |
| What is next | Earning the next open before it is sent | One line |
| Company news, if any | Being findable by the few who care | Two sentences, at the bottom |

## What to measure, and what to ignore

Open rates have quietly stopped meaning much. Privacy features in mail apps load images automatically, which registers as an open by somebody who has not read a word, so a rising open rate can be a change in mail clients rather than a change in interest.

Three things are worth watching instead. Replies, which are the strongest signal a newsletter has a reader. Clicks on the one thing you asked people to do. And unsubscribes, read as a pattern rather than as a verdict on a single send.

A fourth number matters more than all of them and almost nobody tracks it: how many sends in a row actually went out. A newsletter that ran nine months out of twelve is a different asset from one that ran four, regardless of what either scored.

## The rules that apply from the first send

These are not a volume problem that starts later. They apply to an email list of forty.

In the UK and EU, the ICO's guidance on [electronic mail marketing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/electronic-and-telephone-marketing/electronic-mail-marketing/) states that you must not send electronic mail marketing to individuals unless they have specifically consented, with a narrow exception for people who bought or negotiated to buy a similar product or service from you in the past, where you gave them a simple way to opt out both when you first collected their details and in every message since. It also says you must not disguise or conceal your identity, must provide a valid contact address, must offer an opt-out and act on it promptly, and must keep a do not contact list of anyone who unsubscribes.

> [!warning]
> The soft opt-in covers your own past customers, not a list you bought, not the attendees of somebody else's event, and not people who left a business card in a bowl. Those are the three ways small businesses most often get this wrong without meaning to.

The unsubscribe link itself is worth getting right at a technical level. RFC 8058 specifies [one-click unsubscribe](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8058.html), where the List-Unsubscribe-Post header contains the single key and value pair List-Unsubscribe=One-Click. The reason it exists is that anti-spam software often fetches resources in mail header fields automatically, with no action by the user, and there is no mechanical way for a sender to tell an automatic fetch from a real person clicking. Most reputable email platforms handle this for you. It is worth confirming yours does rather than assuming.

## Write it so it can be written

The honest failure point is not the first newsletter. It is the fourth.

The first one gets a free afternoon and enthusiasm. By the fourth, the same person is quoting a job, chasing an invoice and trying to remember what was interesting this month, and the newsletter is the only item on the list with no customer waiting at the other end.

Two things keep it alive. Keep a running note of the small things worth telling people, added to as they happen rather than recalled at the end of the month. And draft the recurring parts in advance so that writing time is spent on the one timely section instead of on starting from nothing.

The cleaning company keeps theirs in a note on a phone. Three lines a week, added in a van. By the time the send date arrives the hard part is choosing which one, and choosing is a two minute job.

## Four things before the next send

None of them take an afternoon.

1. Write the one line answer to why somebody would open it, and if there is no honest answer yet, do not send until there is.
2. Check the list. Everyone on it should be a customer or somebody who asked, with a record of which.
3. Confirm the unsubscribe works, in a real email, by clicking it.
4. Start the running note this week. Three lines, wherever you will actually see them.

Do those four and the fourth newsletter gets written, which is the one that decides whether there is a fifth.
